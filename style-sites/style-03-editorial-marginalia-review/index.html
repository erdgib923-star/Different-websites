The long way round

On reading slowly in a fast decade, and what a margin is for.

By Imogen Achterberg · 14 minute read

There is a pencil in every book I have loved, and most of them are still there. Not the pencil itself, which wanders off the way pencils do, but its traces: a bracket around a sentence, a question mark beside a claim, once, in a library copy I should not have touched, a small drawing of a cat. The margin is the only part of a page that belongs to the reader.

Note 1. A survey in this magazine’s spring issue asked readers when they read. The most common answer was “while waiting for something else”. Sample survey, invented for this demo.

For most of the last decade I read the way the decade asked me to: in the queue, between stations, in the grey minute before a meeting started.1 I finished more books than ever and remember fewer of them. The pages went by like windows on a train.

Slowing down was not a decision so much as an accident. A broken phone, a long autumn, a borrowed copy of a novel too heavy to carry anywhere. I read it at the kitchen table, forty pages a night, and found myself reaching for a pencil for the first time in years.

The margin is the only part of a page that belongs to the reader.

Note 2. The medieval word for these notes was marginalia. Scribes used the space for corrections, complaints about the cold, and the occasional rabbit.

What surprised me was not that I remembered more. It was that I disagreed more. A sentence read quickly is a sentence accepted; a sentence read slowly is a sentence weighed. The pencil marks were mostly small refusals: not quite, says who, too neat.2

A room with wide margins

Every issue of this magazine is built around one idea: that a long piece of writing deserves a long piece of attention, and a page with room to answer it. We print wide margins on purpose. We would like you to write in them.

Note 3. Our editors have a rule: no piece is commissioned about anything less than six months old.

That is also why we publish four times a year and not every day. A quarterly can afford to be late to an argument. It can wait until the noise has moved on and ask what was actually said.3

I do not want to make slow reading into a virtue, or a product. It is only a different speed, and some books are written for it. You can tell them by how they behave when you hurry: they stop making sense. Slow down and they open, the way a stiff door does when you stop pushing and lift.

Slow down and they open, the way a stiff door does when you stop pushing and lift.

So here is the only advice this essay has. Pick one book this season that you cannot finish on a train. Keep a pencil in it. Argue in the margin. When you are done, lend it to someone and do not rub anything out.

The pencil, as ever, is optional. The margin is not.
