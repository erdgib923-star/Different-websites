The Shoalreach Atlas

A coastline that moves: its depths, its tides, its warming water and the shore it keeps giving back to the sea.

Shoalreach is a fictional place. Every map, chart and number here is synthetic sample data, generated in code.

Shoalreach runs for thirty kilometres between Gannet Point and the sands at Ravel. Halfway along, the Kessock Water cuts down from the hills and spreads into a shallow estuary. Three islands sit offshore. Scroll on, and the map beside the text will change as each chapter needs it to.

Chapter 1

The reach

Most of the shore faces south. The towns sit back from the water: Saltmere on the west bank of the estuary, Cairnhead on a rise to the east. Between them the land drops gently, then all at once.

The Kessock Water is the reason the reach exists. It carries silt from the hills and drops it where the current slows, building the banks that give Shoalreach its name.

Annotations on the map mark the estuary mouth and the oldest quay.

Chapter 2

Depth

The colour of the water now shows depth, from five metres at the lightest blue to more than twenty-nine in the darkest. The shelf runs shallow for a long way, then drops toward the open sea past the islands.

One hue, light to dark: darker always means deeper.

Field prints

How this atlas was made

The coast is a height field built from layered value noise. Its contours are traced with d3-contour into the land and depth polygons you scrolled past. Tides are a sum of three synthetic harmonics, temperature anomalies follow a smooth gradient plus noise, and shoreline change is a set of invented erosion rates. None of it is measured. The point is the method, not the numbers.

Colour is part of the method. Depth uses one hue from light to dark. Temperature uses two hues with a grey midpoint. Both scales were checked for colour-blind separation, and every chart has a table below.
